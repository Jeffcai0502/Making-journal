# DES240 Experiment 4: Artificial Intelligence

## Pair Exchange of Live Data Visualisations

At the start of class we did a quick pair exchange about our live data work from last week. I talked through the direction I had taken, how I was using live data, and what I would still like to improve if I had more time. This was useful because it made me explain the project more clearly instead of only thinking about whether it was working technically. Hearing another student talk about their process also reminded me that these weekly experiments are not only about making something that functions, but about showing how the idea developed.

## Overview of Week 4

Week 4 felt a bit different from the earlier weeks. The earlier classes were more focused on making and testing things directly, while this week looked at AI both as a practical tool and as something that needs to be questioned. We moved between hands-on activities and broader discussions about ethics, authorship, data sovereignty, and what it means to use AI in a design process. I think this made the week feel more reflective overall.

One idea that stayed with me was that AI is not just a shortcut for producing outcomes. It can be useful for generating options, summarising information, or helping with technical tasks, but it still needs direction. The better the question or prompt is, the better the result usually is. At the same time, the class also made it clear that speed is not the same thing as depth.

## Local AI with Ollama

In class we tried local AI using Ollama. I downloaded the program, opened the terminal, pulled a small model, and ran it locally on my computer. What I found most interesting was the feeling of difference between local AI and the cloud tools I normally use. Running it locally made the process feel much more direct and visible. It was obvious that the model was working from my own machine rather than from a remote service.

I tested it by asking it a few simple questions and also trying a small creative prompt. It did respond, but the output was noticeably slower and less polished than what I would usually get from a larger online model. That was still useful because it made the trade-off very clear. Local AI gives more control and keeps the interaction on the device, but the result is usually weaker and more limited. Instead of seeing that as a failure, I saw it as a good comparison point.

This also helped me think about AI in a less abstract way. Usually when I use an online tool, I only see the interface and the answer. With Ollama, the process felt more exposed. I could see the model being downloaded, launched, and used through the terminal, which made the technical side of AI feel more real.

## Cloud AI with NotebookLM

After that, we looked at cloud-based AI through NotebookLM. Compared with Ollama, NotebookLM felt much more structured and purpose-built for research. I created a notebook and added materials from my journal and earlier experiments. I also made a short `context.md` file to frame the notebook around three points: the experiment I found most interesting, a theme I keep coming back to, and something I still want to explore further.

That setup step was actually more useful than I expected. It made me summarise my own work in a short and focused way before asking the AI anything. Once I started using the chat, the responses felt more relevant than a normal generic chatbot because they were tied to my own uploaded sources. I asked questions about what themes were appearing across my work and what direction a future project might take. The answers were not perfect, but they were good for reflection.

I also tried the Audio Overview feature. Hearing my work discussed in that format felt strange at first, but it was helpful because it made patterns stand out in a different way. Some parts sounded a bit too general, and some of the detail was simplified, but it still gave me another way to think about my own journal.

## Ethics, Labour, and Data Sovereignty

A major part of this week was not only learning new tools, but also thinking more critically about them. The lecture discussion around dataset bias, hidden labour, and AI ethics made me think about what sits behind these systems. It is easy to focus on convenience and forget that AI tools are built from datasets, classification systems, and large amounts of human labour.

The discussion of data sovereignty and Māori data sovereignty was also important. It shifted the conversation away from AI as just a useful design tool and toward questions of ownership, control, and responsibility. Where data is stored, who has access to it, and whose values shape its use are all part of the design context too. This part of the week made me realise that using AI responsibly is not only about getting correct results, but also about thinking about where the material comes from and who may be affected by it.

## Early Proposal Thinking

This week also linked closely to the upcoming reflective proposal. Looking back across my earlier experiments, I noticed that I am most interested in interactive and responsive forms of data rather than static charts. I keep coming back to the idea that data can be turned into an experience instead of just being shown as information. That is probably the main direction I want to keep exploring.

At the moment, I am especially interested in how digital systems interpret people through behaviour and interaction. I am less interested in using data only to report facts, and more interested in using it to create atmosphere, response, and emotion. Week 4 helped me see that AI can be part of this process, but it should support the design thinking rather than replace it.

## Independent Study: AI-Assisted Data Exploration

For the independent study, I chose a public dataset from data.govt.nz related to support services and wellbeing in New Zealand. I chose this because it connects to a real social issue and also because it seemed like the kind of dataset that could be read in multiple ways depending on how it was presented. It was not only about numbers. It also raised questions about access, visibility, and what kinds of support are easier to find than others.

I uploaded the CSV file into a cloud AI tool and first asked it to explain the structure of the dataset. This was helpful as a first step because it quickly broke down the columns, categories, and gaps in the file. It gave me a broad overview much faster than I would have managed by reading the raw spreadsheet line by line. That made it easier to see possible directions for visualisation.

After that, I asked for different ways of representing the same data. The first outputs were quite predictable and looked like default chart-based solutions. They were useful, but they also felt generic. I then kept changing the prompts to ask for more specific directions, including a clearer audience, a more experimental format, and a stronger design intention. This part taught me that AI tends to default to safe and familiar visual forms unless it is pushed into something more deliberate.

What I found most useful was not any single final result, but the process of comparing outputs. By doing that, I could start to see what the AI assumed by default and what I had to override myself. In that sense, the exercise was less about getting the perfect visualisation and more about learning how to direct, critique, and refine the process.

## Reflection

My main takeaway from Week 4 is that AI works best when it becomes part of a reflective workflow. It is strong at speeding up early stages such as summarising, generating options, and helping me frame questions. It is less strong when I want something original, highly specific, or conceptually sharp without a lot of guidance.

Comparing local AI and cloud AI made this especially clear. Local AI felt more transparent and more controlled, but also weaker. Cloud AI felt more capable and more convenient, but it also raised more questions around dependency, privacy, and where the data goes. I do not think one is simply better than the other. They are useful in different ways.

The independent study also showed me that AI-generated visualisation is not neutral. The form it chooses affects what the viewer notices first and what gets left in the background. That connects strongly to the course ideas around data representation. Even when a dataset looks objective, the way it is organised and visualised still reflects priorities, assumptions, and limits.

Overall, I found Week 4 useful because it balanced practical experimentation with critical thinking. It helped me use AI more consciously rather than just more often. If I had more time, I would keep developing one of the more interactive directions from the independent study and push it further into something that feels less generic and more intentionally designed.

## Brief AI Acknowledgement

AI tools were part of this week's experiment, mainly for testing, comparison, and drafting ideas. I used them as support during the process, but I still had to choose directions, evaluate the outputs, and decide what was worth keeping.
